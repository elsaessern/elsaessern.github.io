A "sniff-test" for gasotransmitters? 

Motivation & background
------
Chemical sensing plays a crucial role for living beings, an important capability to perceive the world and for bodily function. For example, employing the sniff test with my favorite chemical sensor, the nose. Perhaps you've smelled a bit of food to check if it's still safe to eat (or extra flavorful, for the kimchi and sauerkraut lovers out there). Or maybe you left something to cook for a little too long and a smoke detector started beeping. Most people have experienced some form of chemical sensing. However, a particularly important kind of chemical sensing happens at a scale too small for our noses and household devices. Gasotransmitters, small signaling gas molecules, act as messengers in the human body and play essential roles in physiological functions like the nervous, cardiovascular, and immune systems. In [^1] an approach to qualitatively differentiate the gasotransmitters CO, H\\(_2\\)S, NO, and NH\\(_3\\) via covalent organic framework (COF) arrays was investigated. As a non-expert in chemistry, I will refer those interested in COFs and gasotransmitters to the literature. The basic idea of the approach, however, is that different materials (Fe, Co, Cu, Ni-based COFs) can sense different gasotransmitters (CO, NO, H\\(_2\\)S, NH\\(_3\\)) well but have trouble differentiating between certain gases on their own. Hence, one could reasonably ask if a collection of these materials can effectively sense and differentiate gasotransmitters.  

The data
------
In the experiments, the four COFs (Fe, Co, Cu, and Ni based) were exposed to each gas (CO, NO, H\\(_2\\)S, NH\\(_3\\)) for 30 minutes and responses were measured in negative normalized conductance. I.e. there is a time series associated with each COF + gas combination. See figure 1 for an example. With sensor response data in hand, the goal is to qualitatively evaluate the ability to differentiate between the four gases based on the COF array responses. To leverage machine learning to accomplish this goal, we systematically extracted three features from each time series: initial slope of response, area under curve for duration of exposure, and saturation response (the equilibrium in late exposure time). Why these features? This choice has to do with the expected logarithmic-like sensor response (google the Langmuir adsorption model for more details) where initial slope, area under curve, and saturation/limiting value are good characteristic features.

Principal component analysis
------
Since there are four COFs, four gases, and three features per time-series, we encode a 4-COF array response to a gas as a 12-dimensional vector. It is with this high-dimensional data we aim to show that the four-COF array can effectively differentiate the four different gases. To do so, we use principal component analysis (PCA), an unsupervised dimensionality reduction method. In short, PCA uses the spectral theorem on the covariance matrix of our standardized data to obtain an orthonormal basis from which two or three vectors capture most of the variance in the data. Then we plot our data in terms of these two or three vectors to determine if there is meaningful differentiation. In our situation, we have a \\( 12 \times 47 \\) matrix of sensor response features (see figure 2). Each row corresponds to a feature (initial slope, saturation, area under curve) and COF (Fe, Ni, Co, Cu), and each column corresponds to a gas. There are multiple columns for each gas because of multiple experiments/trials. 

<img src='/images/PCAFigure2-heatmap.pdf'>
Figure 2: Matrix of transformed data. Each column corresponds to a different gas and experiment with the feature data from the four COFs. The plot below shows the concentration of the gas in the respective column's experiment. 

Let's call our matrix of data \\(M\\) and recall our goal in PCA: use spectral theorem to obtain a basis from which two or three vectors "capture" the most variance in our data. So we first need to get the covariance matrix from our data \\( M \\). To do this we use a [power transformer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.preprocessing.PowerTransformer.html) to standardize our data (make it gaussian-like). Denote our standardized data matrix by \\( \tilde{M} \\) (this is the matrix in fig 2). Then we can obtain the covariance matrix \\( \Sigma=\frac{1}{47-1}\tilde{M}\tilde{M}^T \\)
which is symmetric and the variance of each feature/row from our data are the diagonal entries. So the trace of \\( \Sigma \\) gives us the total variance from the rows of \\( \tilde{M} \\). Since \\( \Sigma \\) is symmetric, there exists real eigenvalues \\( \lambda_1\geq\ldots\geq \lambda_{12} \\) and an associated basis of orthonormal eigenvectors \\( z_1,\ldots,z_{12} \\) by the spectral theorem. Now we also know that \\( tr(\Sigma)=\lambda_1 + \ldots + \lambda_{12} \\). Then if we take the largest two eigenvalues \\( \lambda_1,\lambda_2 \\), we know they are each some percentage of the total variance. And if their sum is a substantial percentage of the variance (say \\( >90\% \\)), then the subspace \\( span \{ z_1,z_2 \} \\) is a good candidate to investigate separation in our data because of the large proportion of variance captured in the directions \\( z_1 \\) and \\( z_2 \\). So what happens when we do this with our gas sensing data? See figure 3 for the plot of our PCA results. 

<img src='/images/PCAFigure3.pdf'>

Figure 3: Plot of data in terms of the first two principal components. Note the clear separation of gases and that the first two principal components account for 81 and 12.2 percent of the variance respectively. 

We can see that the four gases separate well when plotted with respect to the first two principal components. Moreover, the differentiation becomes stronger with higher concentrations (ppm). This separation gives compelling evidence that the 4-COF array may effectively differentiate the four different gasotransmitters and that training a classification model (such as K-nearest neighbors) may be worth exploring.
